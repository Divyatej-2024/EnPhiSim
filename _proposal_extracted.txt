Introduction: 
Phishing has remained the most specific risk, as it continues to affect our technology 
and our minds. Despite the rapid technological advances and campus safety drills, 
some attacks are unknown to most, like Pop-up Phishing, Angler Phishing, Pharming 
and some more (Aburrous, 2010), these attacks continue to exist unknowingly since 
they are becoming more sophisticated, and they are also exploiting our emotions 
(APWG, 2024). 
My project, which is called EnPhiSim, is aimed at targeting it on both ends: training 
and detection. I am introducing Natural Language Models such as SVM, Random 
Forest, BERT, CNN and many more. I am currently using DistilBERT and CNN to 
identify phishing types and Execution. Moreover, there is a gamified Walkthrough 
simulator to keep us engaged as I train model to identify the flaw actions we take in 
our day-to-day life and a better understanding of how phishing attacks are done. 
Aims and Objectives:

framework that enhances awareness and detects phishing attempts using Natural 
Language Models.

Rationale for the project Choice: 
Phishing attacks represent a major portion of global cyber incidents, leading to data 
breaches, identity theft, and financial loss (APWG, 2024). Many existing simulators 
focus purely on awareness training without real-time AI-based detection, but my 
Simulator focuses on redirection and actions and consequences after every action 
taken along with exploring every type of Phishing attack existing now. Conversely, 
detection systems lack an interactive awareness element (Basnet, 2012). 
EnPhiSim addresses this gap by merging both aspects—awareness and detection—
within a single integrated framework. By combining machine learning, natural 
language processing, and user education, the project contributes to building human 
and technological resilience. Furthermore, it supports academic exploration in 
cybersecurity education and applied AI. 
 
Background research: 
Studies have explored various machine learning approaches to detect phishing, 
including Support Vector Machines (SVMs), Random Forests, and Logistic 
Regression (Basnet, 2012). However, these models often struggle with contextual 
language understanding. Transformer-based architectures such as BERT and 
DistilBERT have outperformed traditional models in semantic analysis and text 
classification due to their deep contextual embedding capabilities (Devlin, 2019). 
Simulators like GoPhish, PhishMe, and KnowBe4 (KnowBe4, 2024) provide 
structured training environments but lack adaptive feedback or integration with AI 
detection mechanisms. EnPhiSim’s approach—combining a DistilBERT  + CNN hybrid 
model with a 32-level gamified walkthrough simulator—introduces adaptability, 
continuous learning, and real-time detection feedback, enhancing both user 
understanding and threat mitigation.

Methodology: 
1. Data Collection: 
Public datasets from PhishTank (Phish Tank, 2024), Kaggle, and UCI will be 
gathered, containing both phishing and legitimate email samples. 
2. Data Preprocessing: 
Cleaning, tokenisation, stop-word removal, and embedding extraction using 
DistilBERT (Devlin, 2019). 
3. Model Development: 
Fine-tuning DistilBERT for text embeddings. 
Training a CNN layer on top for classification. 
Comparing results with baseline models (SVM, Logistic Regression) (Basnet, 
2012). 
4. Testing and Evaluation: 
Dataset split: 80% training, 20% testing. 
Metrics: Accuracy, Precision, Recall, and F1-Score. 
Performance comparison across models (Zhou, 2023). 
5. Simulator Integration: 
Develop a phishing simulator front-end integrated with the trained model. 
Provide users with phishing examples and real-time detection feedback. 
6. Deployment: 
The final system will be deployed on Vercel, Render, Railway, MongoDB, and 
connect them all providing accessibility and scalability.
